1 · Repeating decimals — the step that is the method

One skill, taught before, and it broke in the same place twice. This section rebuilds it around the step that was missing.

By the end: you can convert a repeating decimal to a fraction and say why the subtraction is what does the work.

The subtract step is the method

Multiplying a repeating decimal by a power of ten does one job only: it lines the repeating tails up so they can cancel. It never removes the repeat by itself. The subtraction is what removes it, and without that step nothing has been converted.

Worked example

Convert 0.777… to a fraction.

Let x = 0.777…, so 10x = 7.777….

Stop and look: 7.777… is still repeating. Multiplying has changed nothing about the tail.

Now subtract the two lines. The tails are identical, so they cancel exactly:
10x − x = 7.777… − 0.777… → 9x = 7.

x = 7/9.

The test: if your answer still has a power of ten underneath it — 100, 1000 — you multiplied and stopped. A converted repeating decimal lands over 9, 99 or 999 (or a factor of them), never over 10, 100 or 1000.

Multiplying moved the decimal point. The repeating tail is still there, unchanged. That is exactly why a second line is needed and why the two lines are then subtracted.

2 · Scientific notation — check the coefficient first

The single weakest skill in the record, and the misses share one shape: the operation is done correctly and the coefficient is left out of range.

By the end: you finish every scientific-notation answer by checking the coefficient, in both directions.

Look at the coefficient before you look at anything else

In a × 10n the coefficient a must satisfy 1 ≤ a < 10. Every operation can push it outside that range, in either direction, and the answer is not finished until you have put it back.

Worked example

Too big. (6.8 × 106) + (3.4 × 106). The coefficients add: 6.8 + 3.4 = 10.2, giving 10.2 × 106.

Stop — 10.2 is not below 10. Move the point one place left and the exponent one up: 1.02 × 107.

Too small. 0.8 × 107. Now the coefficient is below 1. Move the point one place right and the exponent one down: 8 × 106.

The habit: the coefficient is the first thing you check, not the last. If an answer in scientific notation is marked wrong, look there before you touch the exponent — that is where it nearly always is.

3 · The minus in front of a bracket

A settled procedure, repeated identically across four attempts — which makes it mechanical, and mechanical faults are the cheapest kind to fix.

By the end: you expand a bracket with a negative in front of it correctly, and you can check it yourself in five seconds.

A minus in front of a bracket belongs to everything inside it

When you expand −3(x + 2), the −3 multiplies both terms, not just the first. Getting the second sign wrong is the most common way a correct method still produces a wrong answer.

Worked example

−3(x + 2) → −3 · x = −3x and −3 · 2 = −6, so the expansion is −3x − 6.

The five-second check. Put x = 0 into both the original and your expansion.
Original: −3(0 + 2) = −6.   Expansion: −3(0) − 6 = −6. They agree, so the expansion is safe.

Try the wrong version the same way: −3x + 6 gives +6 at x = 0. That disagrees with −6, so it cannot be right — and you knew before you solved anything.

The habit: expand each bracket on its own line before you collect anything, and if a minus sits in front of one, substitute x = 0 into both versions. It costs five seconds and it catches the fault every time.
LearnWhich expansion is right?
−3(x + 2) expands to…
The −3 multiplies both terms: −3 · x and −3 · 2. Substituting x = 0 settles it — the original gives −6, and only one of these options does.
GuidedExpand each bracket on its own line

Solve 4(x − 5) − 3(x + 2) = 7.

Step 1 · Expand 4(x − 5). Its constant term is…
Multiply the outside number by the second term inside. Keep the sign that comes with it.
Step 2 · Expand −3(x + 2). Its constant term is…
The number in front is negative and the term inside is positive. What sign does the product carry?
Step 3 · Collect the two constants you just found:
Add them. Both are on the same side of zero, so the total moves further from it.
Step 4 · The x terms give 1x. Final answer · x =
You now have one x, a constant, and a number on the right. Undo the constant.

4 · Evaluate, then classify

The miss here is a sorting habit, not a definition. The definition is already known — it is being applied to the symbol instead of to the number.

By the end: you evaluate a root before deciding what kind of number it is, and you finish a calculation instead of stopping at the parts.

Work out the number first. Then classify it.

A root sign tells you nothing on its own. √49 and √50 look almost identical and land on opposite sides of the line. Sorting by appearance will get one of them wrong every time; sorting by value never does.

Worked example

√49 — what number is it? 7 · 7 = 49, so √49 = 7. And 7 is a fraction of integers (7/1), so it is rational.

√50 — what number is it? 7 · 7 = 49 and 8 · 8 = 64, so nothing whole squares to 50. √50 = 7.0710…, which never ends and never repeats — irrational.

Two questions, in this order: what number is this?  then  can I write that number as a fraction of whole numbers? The second question is meaningless until the first is answered.

Part A · What number is √49?
Which whole number, multiplied by itself, lands on forty-nine?
Part B · So √49 is — type rational or irrational.
You now have a plain whole number. Can a whole number be written as a fraction?
GuidedAnd the one next to it
Part A · √50 sits between two consecutive whole numbers. Type the smaller one.
Square the whole numbers either side until you bracket fifty.
Part B · So √50 is — type rational or irrational.
It is not a whole number, and its decimal never settles into a pattern.

5 · Inequalities — the flip and the circle

Seventeen attempts, ten misses, three separate faults inside them. They are separated here so each one can be checked on its own.

By the end: you solve an inequality, test your own answer, and show the solution on a number line with the right kind of circle.

The flip, the circle, and the number that does not count

An inequality is solved exactly like an equation, with one extra rule and two habits that are easy to lose. Each of the three is checkable in seconds, so none of them has to be remembered on trust.

Worked example

The flip. Solve −3x + 4 ≥ 19. Subtract 4: −3x ≥ 15. Divide by −3 and turn the sign round: x ≤ −5.

The check. Try a number from your answer. x = −6 gives −3(−6) + 4 = 22, and 22 ≥ 19 is true. Now try one that is not: x = 0 gives 4, which is not ≥ 19. The small numbers work, so the sign must point that way.

The circle. ≤ and ≥ include the boundary, so it is drawn closed. < and > exclude it, so it is drawn open. The words in a problem decide this: at most and at least include; more than and fewer than do not.

Never take the flip on trust. Substitute one number from your own answer back into the original. If it does not satisfy it, the sign is the wrong way round — and you have found that out in five seconds instead of in marking.
LearnWhat happens when you divide by a negative?
You divide both sides of −3x ≥ 15 by −3. What happens?
Dividing by a negative reverses the order of the number line, so the direction of the inequality reverses with it. Substituting x = −6 into the original confirms which way it must point.
GuidedOne line at a time, then test it
Step 1 · −3x + 4 ≥ 19. Subtract 4 from both sides: −3x ≥…
Do to the right what you did to the left. The left-hand side keeps its x term.
Step 2 · Divide both sides by −3. The number on the right is now…
Divide the two numbers and keep track of the signs. A positive divided by a negative is negative.
Step 3 · Final answer — which is the solution?
You divided by a negative, so the sign turns round. Test it: a number below −5 should satisfy the original, and it does.
PracticePut it on the number line
Step 1 · The solution is x ≤ −5. Click the number line to plot the boundary point.
Step 2 · Is the circle at that point open or closed?
The bar under the sign is what includes the boundary. −5 itself satisfies x ≤ −5, so it is filled in.
ApplyThe boundary value does not always count
Step 1 · 4(x + 2) > 36. Expand and simplify to 4x >…
Expand the bracket first, then move the constant across.
Step 2 · Divide by 4: x >…
A straightforward division. The sign does not move — you divided by a positive.
Step 3 · Final answer · the smallest whole number that satisfies it:
Substitute your boundary value into the original. Is 36 greater than 36? If not, that value is not a solution and the next whole number is.
ApplyThe goods lift

A goods lift carries at most 400 kg. The operator rides with the load and weighs 70 kg. Each box weighs 25 kg. Let b be the number of boxes.

Step 1 · The load is 70 + 25b and it must not exceed 400. So 25b ≤…
Take the operator's weight off both sides.
Step 2 · Divide by 25: b ≤…
The answer is not a whole number, and that is correct at this stage. Do not round it yet.
Step 3 · Final answer · how many whole boxes can go in?
You cannot load part of a box, and going up would break the limit. Which way does this one have to go?
Step 4 · On a number line, is the circle at that number open or closed — and what in the question tells you?
At most means “this many or fewer”, so the value itself is allowed and the circle is filled in. The words in the question decide the circle, not the shape of the number.

6 · Say why

This is the strongest thing in the record — a written critique that identified a fault, named the rule and corrected the answer. It is used here as a diagnostic, not a reward.

By the end: you can explain what each method is actually doing, and spot the flaw in someone else's reasoning.

Saying why is not decoration — it is the test of whether you own the method

A procedure you can run but cannot explain is a procedure that will drift. Explaining it out loud is the cheapest way to find out which parts you actually hold and which you are copying from memory. This section asks for reasons, not answers.

Worked example

Take 0.777… × 100 = 77.777…. A repeating decimal has been multiplied by 100, and the result is still a repeating decimal — the tail is untouched.

So multiplying can never, on its own, produce a whole number. What it does is manufacture a second line whose tail is identical to the first. Subtracting the two makes the tails cancel, and only then is there a whole number to work with.

The pattern to notice: in all three of this module's methods the same thing is true — the step that feels like tidying up at the end is the step that does the actual work. The subtraction. The coefficient check. The second sign in the bracket.
ExamCritique the claim
Chidi says: “To turn any repeating decimal into a fraction, you multiply it by 100. That always gives you a whole number.” Which correction is right?
The multiplier is chosen from the length of the repeating block, so 0.777… needs 10 and 0.2727… needs 100. And multiplying is never enough on its own: 0.777… × 100 = 77.777…, still repeating. The subtraction is what removes the tail.
ExamWhere is the mistake?
A student computes (6.8 × 106) + (3.4 × 106), writes 10.2 × 106, and is told it is wrong. What should she change?
The arithmetic and the exponent are both right. The only thing outside the rules is the coefficient, and it is the first place to look whenever a scientific-notation answer comes back wrong.
StretchIn your own words
Why does the subtraction step turn a repeating decimal into a fraction? What exactly would go wrong if you multiplied and then stopped? Use an example of your own.
